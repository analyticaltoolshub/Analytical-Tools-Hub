SKU Assignments

Location IDs are unique within each layout. A SKU can be stored in several locations, and a location can hold several SKUs. Available quantities reset before each order; the simulation does not deplete stock across orders.

Shared SKU Demand

Frequency weight represents relative unit-pick demand: a SKU with weight 40 is drawn four times as often as one with weight 10. Probability = SKU weight / sum of all SKU weights. Higher-weight SKUs are fast movers. Enter historical unit picks for a representative period, or relative estimates; zero excludes a SKU. Adding another storage location does not change its demand.

CSV columns: SKU,FrequencyWeight. Matching SKU names update their weights; new names are added. Other demand records and location assignments are retained. Up to 30 unique SKUs; files up to 1 MB. New SKUs must be assigned to locations before simulation. Files are processed in your browser.

Methodology and Limitations

Distance and aisles

A* finds shortest orthogonal paths on a 1 m cell grid around storage locations. Obstacles are expanded by (aisle width - 1 m) / 2; cell centres also retain half the aisle width from walls. Pick faces use the nearest conservative grid cell. Start/end coordinates identify cells, whose centres are at X + 0.5, Y + 0.5 m. Narrow or disconnected access is rejected.

Order generation

Each unit pick is drawn independently with replacement, with probability equal to SKU weight / total weight. Demand is stored once per SKU in the shared catalogue. Every layout receives the same orders. Available quantities reset before each order. An order that exceeds the total available quantity fails explicitly; replenishment and stock depletion across orders are excluded.

Routes and time

Nearest mode chooses each next location by distance and remaining stock. Fixed mode visits the required locations alphabetically from A to Z, with numeric bay ordering (A2 before A10). The baseline allocates units to stocked locations once; repeated picks at each location are combined, then all layouts follow the same sorted stops and quantities. Matching locations and stock are required. Neither method guarantees a globally shortest tour. Travel seconds = metres / speed; total seconds = travel seconds + units x pick time.

Fair comparisons

The shared SKU catalogue, generated orders, speed, and pick time apply to every layout. Location IDs, positions, assignments, and start/end points can differ. Keep dimensions and total available quantities comparable when isolating a placement change. Improvement = 100 x (baseline mean - alternative mean) / baseline mean; negative means worse. Min/max are sampled extremes, not future bounds.

One picker, constant speed, flat floor, and unrestricted two-way aisles are assumed. Congestion, turning delays, ergonomics, rack height, safety regulations, and labour scheduling are excluded. Aisle clearance is a geometric approximation, not a safety certification. Validate with measured warehouse trips and several seeds before committing to a layout.

Limits: six layouts per project, thirty locations per layout, thirty shared demand SKUs, 80 x 80 m, twenty thousand orders, and thirty unit picks per order. Saved results include inputs, seed, method, diagnostics, and sampled metrics.

FAQ

Can the same SKU be stored in multiple locations?

Yes. Create unique location IDs and assign the SKU and available quantity to each. Its frequency weight appears once in Shared SKU Demand. The picker chooses the nearest location with usable stock and can split a pick across locations.

What does frequency weight mean for a fast mover?

A higher weight gives a SKU a higher probability of being requested. Weights 40, 10, and 50 give probabilities 40%, 10%, and 50%; weights 4, 1, and 5 give the same probabilities. Weight is demand, not a location score or stock quantity. Use historical unit picks from the same period where available.

Can induction and drop-off be in different places?

Yes. Clear Start and end are the same point, then position each independently. When they are shared, moving the induction point also moves the drop-off point. Routes finish at the configured end point.

What do the two sample layouts represent?

Layout 1 has longitudinal rack banks and three picking aisles linked by front and rear cross-aisles. Layout 2 has transverse rack banks with side cross-aisles. Both have 30 bays A1-F5, the same 28 by 24 m footprint, matching SKU assignments, and the same induction point. Dimensions and demand are synthetic teaching inputs.

How does Fixed pick sequence compare layouts?

Order contents are randomly sampled, but location visit order is alphabetical. For example, required stops B5, B1, D4, C2, A2 become Start > A2 > B1 > B5 > C2 > D4 > End. Bay numbers sort numerically, so A2 precedes A10. Picks at the same location are combined into one stop, retaining all quantities. Every layout uses the same sorted stops. Nearest eligible location instead chooses each next stop by travel distance.

Does this optimize the warehouse layout automatically?

You create candidate layouts; the simulation compares their picking performance. It does not automatically search all possible placements. Use the results to review proposed arrangements under representative demand.

Why might a wider aisle make a location unreachable?

The model reserves clearance around locations and walls. Move the location, change its pick face, or widen the physical gap. Blocked start/end points and disconnected pick faces cannot produce a valid simulation.

Can I open this without a server?

Yes. Libraries are bundled locally, with a cancellable batch fallback when a local file cannot use Web Workers.

What is stored or uploaded?

No layout data is uploaded. Browser saving is explicit and device-specific. Project JSON preserves editable layouts and the shared demand catalogue; result JSON captures the calculation-time snapshot. Legacy version 1 projects are converted to unique location IDs with their original SKU assignments and ample per-order stock.
